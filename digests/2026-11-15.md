It's a weekend and I found no major fintech story dated 2026-11-15. The items below are the most notable from Nov 8–14 that previous digests haven't covered. Two of them point to the same theme: AI agents spending money on a customer's behalf, and banks being cautious about it.

- **OpenAI launched ChatGPT Wallet, letting its AI agents make purchases with a user's existing cards (Nov 13).** It was built with Visa, Mastercard and American Express. At launch it is limited to US ChatGPT Pro subscribers, rolls out gradually and works inside OpenAI's "dots" agents. The user approves each purchase with a biometric check. The card network then issues a payment token, so neither the agent nor the merchant ever sees the real card number. Reports differ on details: some list Chase as a supported issuer, and debit and virtual cards are reportedly not supported yet. This is the largest consumer test yet of network tokenization for agent-initiated payments. The banks' main concern is liability: no one has settled who is responsible when an agent acts wrongly. In October, JPMorgan, Citi, Schwab and Wells Fargo took similar steps against agents that log in with customer credentials. Coverage describes the UK moves as pre-emptive, and the banks haven't published their exact rules, so treat the details as unconfirmed. Seen alongside ChatGPT Wallet, the picture is that agents are being allowed through card-network tokens while bank-account access stays closed. ## PM Concept of the Day: Risk Triggers and Fallback Plans

Previous digests covered risk registers and risk responses. Two more PMBOK terms make those responses actually usable:

- A **risk trigger** is an early-warning sign that a risk is about to happen or already has. Each trigger should be observable and have an owner watching it.
- A **fallback plan** is "Plan B": what you will do if the primary response, or the contingency plan, fails. Its purpose is to stop the project from improvising in the middle of a crisis.

On the exam, a typical question says "the trigger has occurred" and asks for the next step. The answer is usually to carry out the planned response from the risk register, not to start a new analysis.

ChatGPT Wallet shows why this matters in fintech. Suppose you were building agentic checkout. A risk would be "issuing banks block agent-initiated transactions." A trigger would be decline rates for agent transactions rising above, say, 3% from one issuer. The contingency response would be sending those users to a step-up flow where the customer confirms the purchase themselves. The fallback plan, if that also fails, would be disabling agent checkout for that issuer behind a feature flag. All of this is defined before launch, with a named owner watching the dashboard.

## One Thing to Try Today (10 minutes)

Choose one risk from a fintech product you know, real or imagined. Examples: "the BaaS partner bank gets a consent order," "the KYC vendor's API goes down at peak sign-up," or "regulators delay a rule our roadmap depends on." Write a four-line entry:

1. **Risk:** one sentence, in cause → event → effect form.
2. **Trigger:** a specific, *measurable* signal, with a number and a data source, plus who watches it.
3. **Contingency response:** what you do when the trigger fires.
4. **Fallback plan:** what you do if the contingency doesn't work.

If your trigger contains a vague phrase like "if things look bad," rewrite it until it's something a dashboard alert could fire on. Being able to turn vague worry into a measurable trigger is one of the clearest ways an IT/fintech PM shows value in risk reviews.
