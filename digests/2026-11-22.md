- **The FDIC (the US deposit insurer) proposed a rule that changes how small banks can pay fintech partners for bringing in deposits (board vote Nov 17).** The rule implements part of the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act. It would let well-capitalized banks with under $10B in assets and a CAMELS rating of 1–3 stop counting "qualifying custodial deposits" as brokered, up to 20% of their total liabilities. Brokered deposits face tighter regulatory limits, so this matters for banks that rely on fintech partners. The FDIC estimates that about 4,031 banks could qualify. The limit: if a fee is based on deposit volume or on the number of accounts opened, it counts as a placement fee, and the deposits lose the exclusion. A flat fee for tracking who owns the funds keeps the exclusion. For BaaS partnerships (where a fintech offers accounts through a partner bank), this makes the fee structure in the contract a compliance question, not just a commercial one. Comments will be open for 60 days after the proposal is published in the Federal Register. ([FDIC press release](https://www.fdic.gov/news/press-releases/2026/fdic-board-directors-approves-proposed-rule-custodial-deposits), [FDIC FIL](https://www.fdic.gov/news/financial-institution-letters/2026/notice-proposed-rulemaking-custodial-deposits-implementing), [PYMNTS](https://www.pymnts.com/legal/bank-regulation/2026/fdic-proposal-details-how-banks-can-pay-fintechs-in-deposit-deals/), [Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/economy/policy/articles/fdic-proposes-custodial-deposit-flexibility-214508620.html))

## PM Concept of the Day: Enterprise Environmental Factors (EEFs) vs. Organizational Process Assets (OPAs)

In PMBOK terms, **Enterprise Environmental Factors (EEFs)** are conditions the project team does not control that still constrain or influence the project. Internal EEFs include company culture, infrastructure and staff skills. External EEFs include regulations, market conditions and the legal environment. **Organizational Process Assets (OPAs)** are the plans, processes, templates, policies and knowledge bases the organization owns and can update. Examples are lessons-learned registers, change-control procedures and approved vendor lists. For the PMP exam, a quick test: if the team can edit it, it's an OPA. If the team has to work within it, it's an EEF. Both appear as inputs to almost every planning process. Fintech tie-in: the FDIC's custodial-deposit proposal above is an external EEF for any BaaS program at a bank under $10B, because no one on the project can change it. The bank's partner-contract templates and fee-approval workflow, which would need to drop volume-based fees, are OPAs, and updating them should be a planned scope item with an owner.

## One Thing to Try Today (10 minutes)

Pick a fintech product you use or work near, such as a neobank account, a card program or a payments API. Make two columns, **EEFs** and **OPAs**. List three entries in each that would shape a new feature for it. For example, an EEF could be the "Reg E dispute timelines," and an OPA could be the "sponsor bank's change-approval checklist." Then circle the EEF most likely to change in the next 12 months, and write one sentence on which OPA you'd have to update if it did. Interviewers ask about this kind of constraint mapping, and you'll use it in your first week as a PM.
