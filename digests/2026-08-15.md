## PM Concept of the Day: Three-Point Estimating (PERT)

Three-point estimating derives a single, more defensible duration or cost estimate from three inputs: an **Optimistic** case (O, if everything goes right), a **Most Likely** case (M), and a **Pessimistic** case (P, if things go wrong) — often combined into a weighted PERT formula, (O + 4M + P) / 6, which pulls the estimate toward the most-likely value while still accounting for the tails. It's a heavily tested PMP calculation because it's the standard antidote to single-point estimates, which tend to be overconfident and get blown through the moment a real risk materializes. ESMA's "Report Once" rollout above is a clean example: the report itself gives a range — short-term fixes in 1-2 years, the full framework in 3-5 years — which is effectively an optimistic-to-pessimistic band a PM would collapse into a single planning estimate (say, O=1, M=3, P=5, giving a PERT estimate of exactly 3 years) rather than just picking the middle of the range by eye. In regulated fintech IT delivery, this matters because vendors and compliance teams routinely give duration ranges instead of firm dates, and a PM who can turn "1 to 5 years" into one defensible number — with a stated confidence level — is the one stakeholders trust with the roadmap.

## One Thing to Try Today

Pick a task on your plate with real uncertainty (a migration, an integration, a compliance filing) and write down your own optimistic, most-likely, and pessimistic duration estimates. Run the PERT formula, (O + 4M + P) / 6, and compare the result to the single-point estimate you'd have given off the top of your head — notice which direction it moved and why. This 10-minute exercise is exactly the calculation PMP exam questions build around, and it's a fast way to sanity-check any date a vendor or teammate hands you as a single number.
